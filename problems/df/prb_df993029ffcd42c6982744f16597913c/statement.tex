\section*{UnsolvedMath ID: 700584}
\textbf{Source problem code:} catalogue entry
\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}
For a group \(G\), define its derived series by \(G^{(0)}=G\) and \(G^{(i+1)}=[G^{(i)},G^{(i)}]\), where \([H,H]\) is the subgroup generated by the commutators of elements of \(H\). The group \(G\) is soluble if \(G^{(d)}=\{1\}\) for some integer \(d\geq 0\). It satisfies the maximum condition on normal subgroups if every ascending chain
\[
N_0\leq N_1\leq N_2\leq\cdots,\qquad N_i\trianglelefteq G,
\]
stabilizes: there is an integer \(m\geq 0\) such that \(N_i=N_m\) for every \(i\geq m\).

A finite presentation \(G=\langle X\mid\mathcal{R}\rangle\) has finite sets of generators \(X\) and defining relators \(\mathcal{R}\). Its word problem is insoluble if no algorithm, given an arbitrary finite word in \(X\cup X^{-1}\), always halts and correctly decides whether that word represents the identity in \(G\).

Does there exist a group \(G\) admitting a finite presentation such that \(G\) is soluble, satisfies the maximum condition on normal subgroups, and has insoluble word problem?
\subsection{Short English statement}
Does there exist a finitely presented soluble group in which every ascending chain of normal subgroups stabilizes, but no algorithm decides whether an arbitrary word in its generators represents the identity?
