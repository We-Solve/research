\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}

\paragraph{Shared notation.}Standard number systems are $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$. Specialized notation and index ranges follow the cited source and the definitions below.


In portfolio control, a utility function quantifies preferences over wealth or holdings, and transaction costs charge changes in positions. A no-trade region is the set of states where immediate trading is suboptimal. Small-cost asymptotics study how this region scales as a cost parameter tends to zero. A parallelotope is an affine image of a box; its orientation is determined by its edge or normal directions.

\paragraph{Statement recorded in the supplied source.}
A.10 Ostrov, Daniel

My primary interest in optimal control in multidimensions has come from applications in financial mathematics. For example, I have recently been working (with Jonathan Good-man) on how to determine the best strategy for trading a number of different stocks so that the expected "utility" of the stocks is maximized. (The term "utility" refers to a specified balance between the desires for a higher return from the stocks and a low total variance.) It is known that in the presence of transactions costs (that is, a charge for buying or selling stocks), there is a "hold region" in the multidimensional space of stock prices, and within this region, one should not trade stocks, but on the boundary of the hold region, one trades so that the stocks do not leave this hold region. It is believed, but not known for certain, that in the asymptotic limit as the transaction costs get small, the hold region becomes a multidimensional parallelogram. However, even assuming that this is true, it is not clear what the orientation of the parallelogram should be. \href{https://aimath.org/WWN/optimalcontrol/optimalcontrol.pdf}{[S2]}

\section{Research attempt: A static no-trade parallelogram is not aligned by covariance alone}
The source's dynamic small-cost claim lacks a fully specified price process,
objective and cost model. Choose instead the following explicit static surrogate:
for a positive-definite matrix $H$, target $y$ and $\lambda>0$, minimize
\[
 F(q)=\tfrac12(q-y)^TH(q-y)+\lambda\sum_i|q_i|.
\]
Starting from zero holdings, the no-trade condition $q=0$ is optimal exactly
when $\|Hy\|_\infty\le\lambda$. Indeed
$F(q)-F(0)=q^THq/2-q^THy+\lambda\sum|q_i|$, nonnegative under the stated
inequalities. If a coordinate of $Hy$ has magnitude larger than $\lambda$,
take a sufficiently small step $q$ along that signed coordinate; its negative
linear term dominates the positive quadratic term, so zero is not optimal.
Strict positive definiteness gives uniqueness of the minimizer.

Thus in this surrogate the no-trade set is
$H^{-1}[-\lambda,\lambda]^d$. In two dimensions it is a parallelogram with
edge directions $H^{-1}e_1,H^{-1}e_2$. These generally differ from the
eigendirections of $H$. For
$H=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, its edge
directions are $(2,-1)$ and $(-1,2)$, whose dot product is $-4$, while the
eigendirections $(1,1),(1,-1)$ are orthogonal. Hence choosing covariance
eigenvectors by inspection does not even solve this simpler orientation problem.

Under an invertible coordinate change $q'=Aq$, the quadratic matrix changes
to $A^{-T}HA^{-1}$, but the transaction penalty changes to
$\lambda\|A^{-1}q'\|_1$. Retaining the original axis-aligned $\ell^1$ penalty
after rotating would define another optimization problem. Substituting the
coordinates in $F$ proves these transformation rules directly.

This is a diagnostic of how curvature and the cost geometry jointly determine
orientation. It neither proves that a stochastic-control no-trade region is
asymptotically a parallelogram nor identifies its effective curvature matrix.
