\section{Many Distinct Prime Factors in Primorial-Length Intervals}\label{statement}


\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}

\paragraph{Shared notation.}Unless a section says otherwise, $\N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$,
$\Z$ is the ring of integers, $\Q,\R,\C$ are the rational, real, and complex
fields, $[N]=\{1,\ldots,\lfloor N\rfloor\}$, and $\log$ is the natural
logarithm. For real functions of a parameter tending to infinity,
$f\ll g$ and $f=O(g)$ mean $|f|\leq Cg$ eventually for some fixed $C$;
$f\gg g$ reverses this comparison; $f=o(g)$ means $f/g\to0$;
$f\sim g$ means $f/g\to1$; and $f\asymp g$ means both order bounds.
A subscript on $O$ or $\ll$ specifies allowed constant dependence.
The expression $n^{o(1)}$ in an upper bound means growth smaller than
$n^\epsilon$ for each fixed $\epsilon>0$. Local definitions govern any
exception, finite-field convention, or use of the same symbol for another
object. An asymptotic claim is not automatically an effective algorithm.



Prime factors are counted distinctly: the target is $\omega(m)>k$. Counting with multiplicity would already make the six-integer case elementary and would not match the source's explicitly unresolved case. The interval length is the primorial of the first $k$ primes.

$p_j$ denotes the $j$th positive prime in increasing order, starting with $p_1=2$. The prime-counting function is $\pi(x)=\#\{p\leq x:p\text{ is prime}\}$. Consecutive primes are consecutive in this full sequence. \sref{statement}{1} \sref{statement}{2}

\paragraph{Statement recorded in the supplied source.}
Let $2=p_1<p_2<\cdots$ be the primes and $k\geq 2$. Is it true that, for all sufficiently large $n$, there must exist an integer in $[n,n+p_1\cdots p_k)$ with $>k$ many prime factors? \sref{statement}{1}

\paragraph{Residual task reported by the archive.}

The embedded review (2026-08-17) records: Resolve the k=2 six-integer interval case. \sref{statement}{1}

\subsection{Short English statement}

For a fixed number of initial primes, must every sufficiently late interval of primorial length contain an integer with more distinct prime factors than that number?
