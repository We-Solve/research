\section{When the Small-Prime Part of a Binomial Coefficient Becomes Large}\label{statement}


\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}

\paragraph{Shared notation.}Unless a section says otherwise, $\N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$,
$\Z$ is the ring of integers, $\Q,\R,\C$ are the rational, real, and complex
fields, $[N]=\{1,\ldots,\lfloor N\rfloor\}$, and $\log$ is the natural
logarithm. For real functions of a parameter tending to infinity,
$f\ll g$ and $f=O(g)$ mean $|f|\leq Cg$ eventually for some fixed $C$;
$f\gg g$ reverses this comparison; $f=o(g)$ means $f/g\to0$;
$f\sim g$ means $f/g\to1$; and $f\asymp g$ means both order bounds.
A subscript on $O$ or $\ll$ specifies allowed constant dependence.
The expression $n^{o(1)}$ in an upper bound means growth smaller than
$n^\epsilon$ for each fixed $\epsilon>0$. Local definitions govern any
exception, finite-field convention, or use of the same symbol for another
object. An asymptotic claim is not automatically an effective algorithm.



The factor $u$ includes the full exponents of all prime divisors at most $k$; $v$ includes those larger than $k$. This factorization is unique. The question concerns the least successful index in a row, in the large-$n$ range where it exists. \sref{statement}{1} \sref{statement}{2}

\paragraph{Statement recorded in the supplied source.}
For $0\leq k\leq n$ write $ \binom{n}{k} = uv $ where the only primes dividing $u$ are in $[2,k]$ and the only primes dividing $v$ are in $(k,n]$.
Let $f(n)$ be the smallest $k$ such that $u>n^2$. Give bounds for $f(n)$. \sref{statement}{1}

\paragraph{Residual task reported by the archive.}

The embedded review (2026-08-17) records: Determine the worst-case order of f(n), including the true limsup scale, and resolve the disputed unbounded-logarithmic-limsup claim. \sref{statement}{1}

\subsection{Short English statement}

How far along a binomial row must one go before the component supported on primes below the lower index exceeds the square of the row index?
