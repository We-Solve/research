\section*{Abstract}
For the full recorded range $1\le m\le n$, an integer right inverse of
$[D\ X]$ exists precisely when, for each prime $p$ dividing some $d_i$,
the rows of $X$ indexed by $\{i:p\mid d_i\}$ are linearly independent
over $\F_p$. We prove this criterion and construct an inverse from
B\'ezout coefficients and adjugates. Rowwise coprimality alone fails,
already for $m=n=2$. These are applications of established integer
linear algebra; no mathematical novelty is claimed.

 M=[D\ X]\in\Z^{m\times(m+n)}

 Y\in\Z^{(m+n)\times m},\qquad MY=I_m.

\section{The characterization}
Write $[k]=\{1,\ldots,k\}$ and set
\[
 P=\prod_{i=1}^m d_i,
 \qquad S_p=\{i\in[m]:p\mid d_i\}.
\]
For row and column sets $I,J$, $X_{I,J}$ means the submatrix in
increasing index order. Write $X_{S_p,:}$ for its indicated rows and
all $n$ columns. Empty matrices have rank zero and determinant one;
an empty product equals one. Gcds are nonnegative, and signs and zero
entries are allowed in their arguments.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:criterion}
Under all the original hypotheses, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $M$ has an integer right inverse.
\item Its column lattice is $D\Z^m+X\Z^n=\Z^m$.
\item The gcd of all $m\times m$ minors of $M$ is one; explicitly,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted}
 \gcd_{\substack{I\subseteq[m],\ J\subseteq[n]\\|I|=|J|}}
 \left(\left(\prod_{i\notin I}d_i\right)\det X_{I,J}\right)=1.
\end{equation}
\item For every prime $p\mid P$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:local}
 \rank_{\F_p}(X_{S_p,:}\bmod p)=|S_p|.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Thus factoring $P$ and doing finitely many Gaussian eliminations over
prime fields decides the question. No tests modulo higher prime powers
are required.
\end{theorem}

\begin{lemma}[Standard maximal-minor criterion, with construction]
\label{lem:minors}
Let $A\in\Z^{r\times q}$ with $1\le r\le q$. For each increasing
$r$-element column set $J$, let $E_J\in\Z^{q\times r}$ have those
standard basis columns, and let $A_J=AE_J$. Then $A$ has an integer
right inverse if and only if $\gcd_J\det A_J=1$. Whenever integers
$c_J$ satisfy $\sum_J c_J\det A_J=1$, one such inverse is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:construct}
 R=\sum_J c_J E_J\adj(A_J).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $AR=I_r$, determinant multilinearity gives Cauchy--Binet:
\[
 1=\det(AR)=\sum_{|J|=r}\det(A_J)\det(R_{J,:}).
\]
For completeness, expand each column of $AR$ as a linear combination
of columns of $A$. Terms with a repeated column vanish. Grouping the
remaining terms by their increasing index set $J$, the signed sum of
the coefficients is precisely $\det(R_{J,:})$. The displayed identity
therefore expresses one as an integer combination of the minors.

Conversely, if their gcd is one, the extended Euclidean algorithm,
applied successively to this finite list, supplies the integers $c_J$.
The cofactor expansion gives $A_J\adj(A_J)=\det(A_J)I_r$, including
when $A_J$ is singular. Consequently \eqref{eq:construct} is integral
and $AR=\sum_J c_J\det(A_J)I_r=I_r$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion}]
The columns of an integer right inverse give integer preimages of the
standard basis of $\Z^m$. Conversely, choosing such preimages and
putting them in columns gives an integer right inverse. This proves
the equivalence of (1) and (2); Lemma~\ref{lem:minors} proves (1)$\iff$(3).
To obtain \eqref{eq:weighted}, a maximal minor selects $m-r$ diagonal
columns, indexed by $T\subseteq[m]$, and $r$ columns of $X$, indexed
by $J$. Expansion along the diagonal columns gives
\[
 \pm\left(\prod_{i\in T}d_i\right)\det X_{[m]\setminus T,J}.
\]
Conversely, every pair $I,J$ in \eqref{eq:weighted} arises in this way
by taking $T=[m]\setminus I$. Signs do not affect the gcd.

Let $\delta$ be that gcd. The minor consisting of all diagonal columns
is $P>0$, so $\delta>0$ and $\delta\mid P$. Thus $\delta=1$ if and
only if no prime $p\mid P$ divides every maximal minor. Reduction
modulo $p$ commutes with determinants, and a matrix over a field has
row rank $m$ precisely when some maximal minor is nonzero. Hence (3)
is equivalent to $\rank_{\F_p}(M\bmod p)=m$ for every $p\mid P$.

Fix such a prime. The diagonal columns of $M\bmod p$ span exactly
the coordinate subspace
\[
 W_p=\operatorname{span}_{\F_p}\{e_i:i\notin S_p\}.
\]
After quotienting $\F_p^m$ by $W_p$, the remaining column images are
exactly the columns of $X_{S_p,:}\bmod p$. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rank}
 \rank_{\F_p}(M\bmod p)
   =m-|S_p|+\rank_{\F_p}(X_{S_p,:}\bmod p).
\end{equation}
This can also be seen by subtracting multiples of the nonzero
diagonal columns from each $X$ column, to zero all coordinates outside
$S_p$. Equation~\eqref{eq:rank} proves (3)$\iff$(4). A prime power
cannot divide $\delta=1$; excluding each prime divisor already suffices.
\end{proof}

\section{Constructing a right inverse or an obstruction}
Lemma~\ref{lem:minors} is a finite algorithm: enumerate the maximal
minors, compute their gcd and B\'ezout coefficients, and use
\eqref{eq:construct}. A smaller certificate follows directly from the
prime tests. When \eqref{eq:local} holds, for each $p\mid P$ select a
set $K_p$ of $|S_p|$ columns such that
$\det(X_{S_p,K_p})\not\equiv0\pmod p$. Let $J_p$ select the diagonal
columns outside $S_p$ and these columns of $X$, in their original
order, and put $a_p=\det(M_{:,J_p})$. Then
\[
 a_p=\pm\left(\prod_{i\notin S_p}d_i\right)\det X_{S_p,K_p},
 \qquad p\nmid a_p.
\]
It follows that $\gcd(P,(a_p)_{p\mid P})=1$: a prime divisor of this
gcd would divide $P$ and would contradict its own selected $a_p$.
Use these B\'ezout coefficients in \eqref{eq:construct}, including
the diagonal minor. This requires at most one plus the number of
distinct prime divisors of $P$ selected minors. The minor chosen for
one prime may differ from the one chosen for another.

If a prime test fails, Gaussian elimination supplies a nonzero vector
$\lambda\in\F_p^{S_p}$ with
$\lambda^{\mathsf T}X_{S_p,:}=0$. Extend it by zeros outside $S_p$ to
$a\in\F_p^m$. Then $a^{\mathsf T}M=0$, while $a\ne0$; an integer
identity $MY=I_m$ would reduce to
$a^{\mathsf T}=a^{\mathsf T}MY=0$, a contradiction. This is a
directly checkable failure certificate.

Existence does not require any single maximal minor to be $\pm1$.
For example, take $m=n=2$ and
\[
 D=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix},\quad
 X=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
 M=\begin{pmatrix}2&0&1&0\\0&3&1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
In lexicographic column-set order the maximal minors are
$6,2,0,-3,0,0$, with gcd one. Direct multiplication verifies
\[
 Y=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\-1&1\\3&-2\\0&0\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad MY=I_2.
\]

\section{The rowwise condition}
Necessity follows by looking at the $(i,i)$ entry of $MY$:
\[
 1=d_iY_{i,i}+\sum_{j=1}^n X_{ij}Y_{m+j,i}.
\]
Every common divisor of the indicated row entries must divide one.
However, rowwise coprimality only ensures that each individual row
in $S_p$ is nonzero modulo $p$; it does not ensure their independence.

An explicit counterexample meeting every original hypothesis is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:counterexample}
 m=n=2,\qquad D=2I_2,\qquad
 X=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 M=\begin{pmatrix}2&0&1&0\\0&2&1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Each row has gcd one. For any integer column
$v=(a,b,c,e)^{\mathsf T}$,
$Mv=(2a+c,2b+c)^{\mathsf T}$ has coordinates of the same parity.
It cannot equal $e_1=(1,0)^{\mathsf T}$, so no right inverse exists.
Equivalently, $S_2=\{1,2\}$ and $X_{S_2,:}$ has rank one modulo two;
the maximal-minor gcd is two.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:rowwise}
Fix any original diagonal matrix $D$ and any $n\ge m$. The rowwise
condition is sufficient for every $X\in\Z^{m\times n}$ if and only
if the $d_i$ are pairwise coprime. In particular, for $m=1$ it is
necessary and sufficient for every $n\ge1$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Pairwise coprimality means $|S_p|\le1$ for every prime $p$. The rowwise
condition makes this single row nonzero modulo $p$, so all the tests
in Theorem~\ref{thm:criterion} pass. For $m=1$, this is exactly
the ordinary B\'ezout condition.

If $p$ divides two diagonal entries, choose every row of $X$ equal
to $(1,0,\ldots,0)$. All row gcds are one, but the rows indexed by
$S_p$ have rank one, less than $|S_p|$. This proves the converse
for every allowed $n$. It also gives failures in every dimension
$m\ge2$, for example with $D=2I_m$.
\end{proof}
Noncoprime diagonal entries do not prevent existence for particular
$X$: taking $X=[I_m\ 0]$ always gives the inverse with zero top block
and bottom block $\begin{pmatrix}I_m\\0\end{pmatrix}$. On the other
hand, $X=0$ never works under $d_i\ge2$. These cases, $m=n$, singular
$X$, and arbitrary prime powers are all covered by the same theorem.

\section{Smith normal form and all inverses}
For comparison with the established literature, $\Z$ is a principal
ideal domain and $M$ has rational row rank $m$ because $P\ne0$.
The standard Smith theorem gives unimodular integer matrices
$U\in\Z^{m\times m}$ and $V\in\Z^{(m+n)\times(m+n)}$ such that
\[
 UMV=[\diag(s_1,\ldots,s_m)\ 0],
 \qquad 1\le s_1\mid s_2\mid\cdots\mid s_m.
\]
Existence is Theorem 2.1(4) of~\cite{stanley}; Theorem 2.4 states
that $s_1\cdots s_m$ equals the gcd of the maximal minors.
Their hypotheses apply over $\Z$, and all $s_i$ are nonzero by the
rank observation. Thus the criterion is precisely $s_1=\cdots=s_m=1$
(equivalently $s_m=1$). Indeed, transforming any right inverse yields
$[\diag(s_i)\ 0](V^{-1}YU^{-1})=I_m$, forcing $s_i=1$.

When the criterion holds, the complete set of integer right inverses is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:all}
 Y=V\begin{pmatrix}I_m\\Z\end{pmatrix}U,
 \qquad Z\in\Z^{n\times m}\ \text{arbitrary}.
\end{equation}
To verify both directions, $MY=U^{-1}[I_m\ 0]\begin{pmatrix}I_m\\Z\end{pmatrix}U
=I_m$, and any inverse makes the top $m$ rows of $V^{-1}YU^{-1}$
equal to $I_m$. This supplementary description uses Smith existence;
the substantive prime criterion and adjugate construction above have
an elementary proof independent of that theorem.
