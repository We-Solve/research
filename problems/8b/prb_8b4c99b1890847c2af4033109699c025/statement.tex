\section{Density Zero under Inverse Proper-Divisor Sums}\label{statement}


\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}

\paragraph{Shared notation.}Unless a section says otherwise, $\N=\{1,2,\ldots\}$,
$\Z$ is the ring of integers, $\Q,\R,\C$ are the rational, real, and complex
fields, $[N]=\{1,\ldots,\lfloor N\rfloor\}$, and $\log$ is the natural
logarithm. For real functions of a parameter tending to infinity,
$f\ll g$ and $f=O(g)$ mean $|f|\leq Cg$ eventually for some fixed $C$;
$f\gg g$ reverses this comparison; $f=o(g)$ means $f/g\to0$;
$f\sim g$ means $f/g\to1$; and $f\asymp g$ means both order bounds.
A subscript on $O$ or $\ll$ specifies allowed constant dependence.
The expression $n^{o(1)}$ in an upper bound means growth smaller than
$n^\epsilon$ for each fixed $\epsilon>0$. Local definitions govern any
exception, finite-field convention, or use of the same symbol for another
object. An asymptotic claim is not automatically an effective algorithm.



For an integer set $A$, its natural density is $\lim_{X\to\infty}|A\cap[1,X]|/X$, when this limit exists. Upper and lower densities use limit superior and limit inferior. A request for density is not automatically satisfied by positive upper density alone.

The divisor-sum function is $\sigma(n)=\sum_{d\mid n,\,d>0}d$. Functional iteration $\sigma(\sigma(n))$ differs from a divisor-power sum such as $\sum_{d\mid n}d^2$; the local subscript definition determines which is intended. \sref{statement}{1} \sref{statement}{2}

\paragraph{Statement recorded in the supplied source.}
Let $ s(n)=\sigma(n)-n=\sum_{\substack{d\mid n\\ d<n}}d $ be the sum of proper divisors function.
If $A\subset \mathbb{N}$ has density $0$ then $s^{-1}(A)$ must also have density $0$. \sref{statement}{1}

\paragraph{Residual task reported by the archive.}

The embedded review (2026-08-17) records: Remove the strong thinness or structural assumptions and prove the assertion for an arbitrary set of asymptotic density zero. \sref{statement}{1}

\subsection{Short English statement}

Does the set of integers whose proper-divisor sum belongs to a density-zero set necessarily also have density zero?
