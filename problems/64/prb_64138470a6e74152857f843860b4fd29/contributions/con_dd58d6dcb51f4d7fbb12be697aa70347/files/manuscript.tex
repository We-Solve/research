\subsection{Recovered rules and one-dimensional strategy}
The catalogue excerpt omits legal-move details. The original report supplies them: every vertex altitude of the full-dimensional simplex is at least $\delta>0$, all vertices lie in the unit ball, and a replacement crosses the affine hyperplane of the remaining face while preserving these properties. We use these source rules, without editing the preserved excerpt.
In dimension one the simplex is an interval $[a,b]\subseteq[-1,1]$ of length at least $\delta$. If it does not contain zero, reflect signs if necessary so that $0<a<b$. Peter removes the farther endpoint $b$.
\begin{proposition}
For $d=1$, this strategy forces termination within $\lceil1/\delta\rceil$ rounds, whenever the initial interval does not already contain zero.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A legal replacement is an endpoint $e<a$ with $a-e\ge\delta$. Such a replacement exists: $e=a-\delta$ lies in $[-1,1]$, because $0<a<b\le1$ and $\delta\le b-a<1$. If $e\le0$, the new interval contains zero. Otherwise the new positive interval is $[e,a]$, and its largest endpoint $a$ is at most $b-\delta$. Thus after $r$ nonterminating rounds its largest endpoint is at most $1-r\delta$ and is strictly positive. There cannot be $\lceil1/\delta\rceil$ nonterminating rounds. Reflection gives the same argument for negative intervals.
\end{proof}
This proves the expected elementary one-dimensional case, not a new general-game strategy. In higher dimensions a vertex opposite a selected face is not ordered by a single scalar endpoint, and the proof gives no rule ensuring a uniform decrease.
\subsection{Verification and remaining gaps}
For $\delta=1/4$, the legal sequence $[3/4,1]\to[1/2,3/4]\to[1/4,1/2]\to[0,1/4]$ terminates in three rounds. Every length is $1/4$ and every nonterminal largest endpoint drops by $1/4$. No bound for arbitrary $d$ is established.
