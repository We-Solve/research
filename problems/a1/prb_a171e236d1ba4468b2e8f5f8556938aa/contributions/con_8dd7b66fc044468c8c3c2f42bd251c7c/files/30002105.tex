\subsection{Scale invariance and a finite geometric ambiguity}
The primary report uses an undirected unweighted neighbor graph; the preserved catalogue also explains the directed convention. The following obstructions apply to either convention and leave that distinction explicit. Later primary work studies density recovery and synchronization-based localization under additional assumptions. We test whether the graph alone can supply an absolute scale and whether every finite neighbor pattern determines a geometry.
\begin{proposition}
Let $p$ be a density on $\mathbb R^d$ and $a>0$. The coupled samples $Y_i=aX_i$ have density $p_a(y)=a^{-d}p(y/a)$ and exactly the same nearest-neighbor adjacency as the $X_i$. Consequently no graph-only estimator can estimate absolute density values with one fixed multiplicative factor $C\ge1$, uniformly over all rescalings, with success probability greater than $1/2$ at a fixed sample index where $p(X_i)>0$ almost surely.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Change of variables gives the stated density. Every interpoint distance is multiplied by $a$, preserving its order and any consistent tie convention. Couple the estimator's random seed as well, so its output $T_i$ is identical on the two graphs. The permitted intervals for successful estimation in the two models are
\[
[p(X_i)/C,Cp(X_i)]\quad\hbox{and}\quad
[a^{-d}p(X_i)/C,Ca^{-d}p(X_i)].
\]
Choose $a^d>C^2$. These intervals are disjoint on every coupled sample with positive density. The two success indicators therefore sum to at most one, and the same is true of their probabilities. They cannot both exceed $1/2$.
\end{proof}
This excludes only a uniform absolute-scale guarantee. Density ratios or recovery up to an unknown common multiplicative normalization do not have this obstruction, and ``up to constant factors'' might intend such a normalization. Thus the argument does not refute the intended density-recovery results.
For a separate finite example, the labeled configurations $(0,1,10,11)$ and $(0,1,100,101)$ on the line have identical directed one-nearest-neighbor graphs: the first two points choose each other, as do the last two. Their undirected versions are also identical. They are not related by a labeled similarity $x\mapsto ax+b$ with $a\ne0$: the first two coordinates force $b=0,a=1$, after which the third coordinates disagree. The invariant ratio of the third-to-first distance to the second-to-first distance is 10 versus 100.
