\section{Research attempt: Exact selector examples and a robust identity submatrix}

\paragraph{The matrix and an elementary PSD-rank lemma.}
For $f:\{0,1\}^n\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}$, the rows are $i=(i_1,\ldots,i_n)\in[m]^n$, the columns are $x=(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(n)})\in(\{0,1\}^m)^n$, and
\[
 M_f(i,x)=f(x^{(1)}_{i_1},\ldots,x^{(n)}_{i_n}).
\]
The PSD rank of the $R\times R$ identity matrix is exactly $R$. The upper bound uses diagonal coordinate projections. For the lower bound, suppose $\delta_{ij}=\operatorname{tr}(A_iB_j)$ with real symmetric positive semidefinite $r\times r$ matrices. Spectral square roots give
\[
 \operatorname{tr}(A_iB_j)=\|A_i^{1/2}B_j^{1/2}\|_F^2.
\]
For $i\ne j$ this is zero, so the ranges of $A_i$ and $B_j$ are orthogonal. For $i=j$ it is nonzero, so choose $a_i$ in the range of $A_i$ and $b_i$ in the range of $B_i$ with $\langle a_i,b_i\rangle\ne0$. If $\sum_i c_i a_i=0$, pairing with $b_j$ forces $c_j=0$. Hence $r\ge R$. Restriction to a submatrix cannot increase PSD rank, since the same factors remain valid.

\paragraph{Two selectors computed exactly.}
For $f(z)=z_1$ and $n\ge1$, the matrix depends only on the first row index and first column block. It contains $I_m$ by choosing first blocks equal to singleton indicator vectors. Conversely $A_i=e_{i_1}e_{i_1}^T$ and $B_x=\operatorname{diag}(x^{(1)})$ give an $m$-dimensional factorization. Thus $\operatorname{rank}_{\mathrm{psd}}M_f=m$.

For $f(z)=\prod_{j=1}^n z_j$, take columns indexed by $a\in[m]^n$, with each block $x^{(j)}$ the singleton indicator of $a_j$. Then $M_f(i,x)=1$ exactly when $i=a$, giving $I_{m^n}$. Since the matrix has $R=m^n$ rows, a diagonal factorization of dimension $R$ always exists: use the row coordinate projections and, for each column, its entries on the diagonal. Therefore
\[
 \operatorname{rank}_{\mathrm{psd}}M_{\mathrm{AND}_n}=m^n.
\]
Here SOS degree means the maximum degree of the polynomials being squared, rather than twice that degree. This example has exact SOS degree $n$ when squares of multilinear polynomials on the Boolean cube are used. If $\mathrm{AND}_n=\sum_jq_j^2$, every $q_j$ vanishes at every Boolean point except $(1,\ldots,1)$. The unique multilinear function with these values is $q_j=c_j\prod_i z_i$. Uniqueness follows by induction on the variables: a multilinear polynomial is $u(z')+z_n v(z')$, determined by its restrictions at $z_n=0,1$. At least one $c_j$ is nonzero, forcing degree $n$; the reverse bound follows by squaring $\mathrm{AND}_n$ itself on the cube.

\paragraph{The lower bound survives a constant perturbation in this example.}
For $f=\alpha\mathrm{AND}_n+\epsilon$ with $\alpha>0$, $\epsilon\ge0$, the same submatrix is $\alpha I_R+\epsilon J_R$, of ordinary rank $R$: on the all-ones vector its eigenvalue is $\alpha+\epsilon R$, and on its orthogonal complement it is $\alpha$. A PSD factorization of size $r$ places every row in the span of the $r(r+1)/2$ coordinate functions of a symmetric matrix under the trace pairing. Thus ordinary rank is at most $r(r+1)/2$, proving
\[
 r\ge \frac{\sqrt{1+8m^n}-1}{2}.
\]
Taking $m=n$ gives $n^{\Omega(n)}$ for this particular function, even with the perturbation. Normalizing its expectation by a positive scalar changes no PSD rank: multiply all row factors by that scalar. This is an elementary special-family calculation, not the requested lifting theorem for every admissible $f$.

\paragraph{A quantifier boundary.}
For $m=1$ there is only one row, so every nonzero nonnegative selector matrix has PSD rank one. Thus a general lower bound cannot be read as holding for every positive $m$ merely because $m\le n^{O(1)}$. The intended statement must select a sufficiently large polynomial gadget size, with the correct dependence on $f$ and its approximation measure. A successful identity-submatrix argument for AND supplies neither that dependence nor arbitrary-function coverage.
