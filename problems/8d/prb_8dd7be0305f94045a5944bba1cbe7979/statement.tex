\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}
For a group \(G\), define its derived series by \(G^{(0)}=G\) and \(G^{(i+1)}=[G^{(i)},G^{(i)}]\), where \([H,H]\) is the subgroup generated by all commutators of elements of \(H\). The group \(G\) is soluble if \(G^{(d)}=\{1\}\) for some integer \(d\geq 0\). It is finitely presented if it admits a presentation with finitely many generators and finitely many defining relations.

Using the conventional interpretation of finite rank as finite Pr\"ufer rank, \(G\) has finite rank if there exists an integer \(r\geq 0\) such that every finitely generated subgroup of \(G\) can be generated by at most \(r\) elements. The supplied record does not define rank; the formulation below is conditional on this interpretation.

\textbf{Problem.} Does there exist a single algorithm which, given any two finite presentations \(P\) and \(Q\), under the promise that their presented groups \(G_P\) and \(G_Q\) are soluble and have finite rank, halts and correctly decides whether
\[
G_P\cong G_Q?
\]
The derived lengths and rank bounds may vary with the input groups; no such bounds are supplied as additional input. This is the usual promised-input interpretation of the isomorphism problem for this class. It imposes no requirement to recognize membership in the class or to halt on inputs outside it.
\subsection{Short English statement}
Is there an algorithm deciding whether two finitely presented soluble groups of finite rank are isomorphic, when both input groups are promised to belong to this class? Here finite rank is interpreted as finite Pr\"ufer rank, subject to confirmation of the source's terminology.
