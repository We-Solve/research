\section*{Adler's renewal question}\label{statement}

\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}
A two-sided sofic shift $X\subset A^{\mathbb Z}$ is the set of
label sequences of bi-infinite paths in a finite labelled
directed graph. It is irreducible if any two words in its
language can be joined by a finite intervening word.
The alphabet is finite, the topology is the product topology,
and the left shift is $(\sigma x)_i=x_{i+1}$.

For a finite set $\mathcal W$ of nonempty words over a finite
alphabet $B$, the renewal shift $X(\mathcal W)$ consists of
all bi-infinite concatenations of these words, with arbitrary
placement of the origin. Precisely, $y\in X(\mathcal W)$ if
there are integers $b_j\to\pm\infty$ as $j\to\pm\infty$,
strictly increasing in $j$, such that
$y_{[b_j,b_{j+1})}\in\mathcal W$ for every $j$.

The catalogue asks whether every irreducible sofic shift is
conjugate to a renewal shift. That general assertion is false:
Williams constructed a strictly sofic counterexample. Retain
here Adler's remaining finite-type question, explicitly stated
in the cited primary treatment:

Is every irreducible shift of finite type conjugate to some
$X(\mathcal W)$? A shift is of finite type if a finite list
of forbidden words defines it. The required conjugacy is a
homeomorphism $H:X\to X(\mathcal W)$ with $H\sigma=\sigma H$.
The alphabet and coordinates may change, but the generating
word list must remain finite. Concatenation need not be unique.
This retained subclass is a proper restriction of the
historical sofic question, whose negative answer is not being
presented as an unresolved target.
\subsection{Short English statement}
Can every irreducible shift of finite type be recoded as concatenations of finitely many words? The broader sofic assertion has a counterexample.
