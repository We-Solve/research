\section{Recovered oracle and the two separate targets}
The primary report, printed pages 2711--2713, specifies quantum
binary entry-oracle access and classical diagonal scaling factors;
it already states nontrivial precision-dependent upper and lower
bounds. Its two final questions concern improved high-precision
matrix scaling and the remaining gap for estimating all row sums.
The related STACS2022 primary abstract was inspected for context,
not used as a substitute for proving a new bound. We use a
finite binary entry representation and require a classical output
vector $\widehat r$ with $\|\widehat r-r\|_1\le\epsilon$ with
probability at least $2/3$. Dense here refers to the $n$ by $n$
entry-address space; entries may be zero as the source permits.
The following known-style elementary bounds are weaker than the
source's strongest bounds and count as no original-target progress.

\section{A complete constant-error search lower bound}
There are $N=n^2$ entry addresses. Restrict inputs to the zero
matrix and matrices with a single entry equal to one. All have
total mass at most one. If $\epsilon<1/2$, a successful row-sum
estimate distinguishes these two cases by testing whether
$\sum_i\widehat r_i>1/2$: on success that sum differs from the
true total by at most $\epsilon$. A binary entry query on this
family is exactly a query to a string that is zero everywhere
or has one marked address.

For completeness, here is a hybrid proof of an $\Omega(\sqrt N)$
quantum query bound. Purify the algorithm's random choices and
defer its measurements into ancillas; this adds no input queries
and preserves its output probabilities. Let $p_t(z)$ be the
probability weight on address $z$ just before query $t$ in its
all-zero run. Let $d_z$ be the Euclidean distance between final
pure states on the zero input and the input marked at $z$.
Changing that query oracle changes any state by norm at most
twice the norm of its component at address $z$. A telescoping
hybrid, using the zero-input prefix and the marked-input suffix
at the differing query, and preservation of norms by all later
unitaries, therefore gives
\[
 d_z\le2\sum_{t=1}^T\sqrt{p_t(z)},\qquad
 \sum_z d_z^2\le4T\sum_{t,z}p_t(z)=4T^2.
\]
The second inequality is Cauchy--Schwarz, and each query state's
address weights sum to one. The success promise makes the
acceptance probability for the nonzero case differ by at least
$1/3$ on each marked input. For unit vectors $u,v$ and a
measurement effect $0\preceq E\preceq I$,
\[
 |\langle u,Eu\rangle-\langle v,Ev\rangle|
 \le|\langle u-v,Eu\rangle|+|\langle v,E(u-v)\rangle|
 \le2\|u-v\|.
\]
Hence $d_z\ge1/6$ for every $z$, giving
$T\ge\sqrt N/12=n/12$. The constants are not optimized.
This is not the stronger constant-precision lower bound already
reported in the source.

\section{Exact precision saturation from parity}
There is a second elementary regime with a matching trivial
upper bound. Let every entry be $A_{ij}=b_{ij}/N$ for arbitrary
bits $b_{ij}$. If $\epsilon<1/(2N)$, rounding
$N\sum_i\widehat r_i$ recovers the integer Hamming weight
$\sum_{ij}b_{ij}$ on every successful run, and hence its parity.
The entry oracle on this family can be simulated with one bit
query. In the binary XOR response register let $w$ be the known
nonzero word encoding $1/N$, with zero encoded by the zero word.
Choose an input-independent invertible linear change of response
bits sending $w$ to the first basis vector. Apply that change,
query the input bit into the first response bit, and undo the
change. The net response update is exactly XOR by $b_{ij}w$.
A different fixed zero encoding can first be removed by a known
XOR, with no input queries.

A $T$-query quantum bit algorithm has acceptance probability
that, on Boolean inputs, is a multilinear polynomial of degree
at most $2T$. To prove this directly, each computational-basis
amplitude starts independent of the bits. A bit query replaces
an amplitude by $(1-b_z)$ times one preceding amplitude plus
$b_z$ times the amplitude with the response bit flipped, raising
its polynomial degree by at most one. Input-independent unitaries
only form linear combinations. After $T$ queries amplitudes have
degree at most $T$, and summing squared moduli for an output
measurement gives degree at most $2T$. Replacing $b_z^k$ by
$b_z$ makes the polynomial multilinear without increasing degree.
Ancillas or deferred measurements do not change the argument.

Use signs $z_i\in\{-1,1\}$ and let
$\chi(z)=\prod_{i=1}^N z_i$. If a parity algorithm succeeds
with probability at least $2/3$ on every input, its acceptance
polynomial $p$ can be labelled so that
$\chi(z)(2p(z)-1)\ge1/3$. But every polynomial of degree less
than $N$ has zero uniform correlation with $\chi$: each of its
multilinear monomials misses a sign coordinate, whose independent
mean is zero. Thus $2T\ge N$. Querying all $N$ entries and
summing them gives an exact row vector, so this restricted
high-precision regime has query complexity $\Theta(n^2)$.
This is a rederivation of an elementary saturation regime, not
an improvement of the source's precision tradeoffs. Exact
arithmetic/output costs are separate from query counts. At the
other endpoint $\epsilon\ge1$, the zero output always suffices.

\section{Why an additive row estimate does not finish scaling}
Suppose current positive row sums are $s_i$, target row masses
are $r_i\ge0$ with total one, and estimates satisfy
$|\widehat s_i-s_i|\le\delta s_i$ for $0<\delta<1$.
Rescaling row $i$ by $r_i/\widehat s_i$ leaves true row sum
$r_i s_i/\widehat s_i$ and therefore total row error at most
\[
 \sum_i r_i\frac{|s_i-\widehat s_i|}{\widehat s_i}
 \le\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}.
\]
This proves a relative-error one-step guarantee. Pure additive
$\ell_1$ accuracy does not imply it. For $0<\eta<1$, take
\[
 A=\operatorname{diag}(\eta^2,1-\eta^2),\quad
 \widehat s=(\eta,1-\eta^2),\quad r=(1/2,1/2).
\]
The row estimate has $\ell_1$ error $\eta-\eta^2\le\eta$,
yet its prescribed row rescaling gives true row sums
$(\eta/2,1/2)$ and error $(1-\eta)/2$, which stays near
one half as $\eta\downarrow0$. The input matrix is positive
on its diagonal and is exactly scalable to these target
marginals; the problem here is the approximate update, not
scalability. The estimates are allowed to be nonnormalized, as
in the row-sum query task. Normalizing is an extra operation
whose guarantee would require its own analysis.
