\paragraph{Shared notation.}Standard number systems are denoted by $\mathbb N,\mathbb Z,\mathbb Q,\mathbb R,\mathbb C$; index ranges, computational models and specialized notation follow the cited problem source and the definitions below.


For an integer matrix $A$, the fiber at $b$ is $\mathcal F_b=\{x\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^d:Ax=b\}$. A Markov basis is a finite set of integer moves $u$ with $Au=0$ such that steps $x\mapsto x\pm u$ staying nonnegative connect every nonempty fiber. A margin is a sum over selected table indices; a conditional probability divides a joint cell probability by an appropriate margin. Rounding such summaries can replace one exact fiber by several compatible fibers or make the imposed summaries inconsistent.

\subsection{Verbatim supplied catalogue question}
Question 12. What can be done with Markov bases when their are floating point approxi-mations made in marginals or conditionals?

\section{A specified interval interpretation}
Fix integer $A\in\mathbb Z^{r\times d}$ and integer bounds $L\le U$.
Investigate $S=\{x\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^d:L\le Ax\le U\}$, with a
fixed total constraint included if finiteness is needed for sampling.
For a stated real reporting interval $l\le Ax\le u$, integer outputs
make it equivalent to $\lceil l\rceil\le Ax\le\lfloor u\rfloor$.
This assumes the endpoints themselves are rigorously specified; a printed
floating-point value without a rounding rule does not determine them.

\paragraph{Exact bijective lift.}
Define $s=Ax-L$ and $t=U-Ax$. Then $x\in S$ exactly when
$(x,s,t)\ge0$ is an integer solution of
\[
\begin{pmatrix}A&-I&0\\A&0&I\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x\\s\\t\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}L\\U\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The lift is unique for every $x$, as the two equations solve for both
slacks. Thus it is a bijection with one exact nonnegative integer fiber.
If a Markov basis for this augmented matrix is given, project its moves
onto $x$. A nonnegative lifted path projects to a feasible path in $S$,
and any two points of $S$ lift to points connected by that basis.
There is no nonzero move involving only slacks: setting its $x$ part
to zero in the two homogeneous equations forces both slack parts zero.
This proves conditional connectivity, but computing a usable augmented
basis remains a separate problem.

\paragraph{Why the original exact-fiber basis cannot suffice.}
Take $A=(1\ 1)$ and $1\le x_1+x_2\le2$. Every move in $\ker A$
preserves $x_1+x_2$. Therefore no sequence of such moves can connect
$(1,0)$ to $(2,0)$, although both satisfy the reported interval.
Simply reusing a basis for exact margins fails on this smallest example.
Rounding combines different fibers and requires moves that can change
the reported statistic inside its permitted range.

\paragraph{Conditional probabilities and target distributions.}
For a specified conditional report $a/b\le x_j/m\le c/d$ with positive
integers $b,d$ and denominator $m=\sum_{j\in J}x_j>0$, multiplication
gives integral inequalities $bx_j-am\ge0$ and $cm-dx_j\ge0$.
One must also impose $m\ge1$. Thus specified rational interval reports
can be handled by a similar inequality-and-slack construction. Dropping
the positive-denominator constraint would introduce undefined conditional
probabilities. Strict integer inequalities can be tightened by one unit.
The bijection does not choose a probability law: if a target weight
$w(x)$ is specified, assign the same weight to its unique lift. Uniform
slack-fiber sampling then corresponds to uniform $x$ only when that is
the intended target, not automatically for a statistical null model.
