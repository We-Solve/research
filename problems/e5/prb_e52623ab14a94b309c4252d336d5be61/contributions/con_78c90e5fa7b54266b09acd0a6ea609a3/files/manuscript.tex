\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}
For $A\in\mathbb C^{M\times N}$ and an integer $s\ge1$, define the
restricted isometry constant
\[
 \delta_s(A)=\sup_{\substack{x\ne0\\|\operatorname{supp}(x)|\le s}}
             \left|\frac{\|Ax\|_2^2}{\|x\|_2^2}-1\right|.
\]
Here $\operatorname{supp}(x)=\{j:x_j\ne0\}$; the same definition applies
to real matrices and vectors. Thus $(s,1/3)$-RIP means
$(2/3)\|x\|_2^2\le\|Ax\|_2^2\le(4/3)\|x\|_2^2$ simultaneously
for every vector supported on at most $s$ coordinates.

Construct explicit deterministic rectangular matrices, with $M<N$,
that achieve the source's target scale
\[
                  s\ \gtrsim\ \frac{M^{3/5}}{(\log N)^C}
\]
for an absolute constant $C$, in nonvacuous growing parameter ranges.
The hidden positive constant is independent of $M,N$. The requested
construction should produce the matrix in polynomial time in its
output dimensions, with a specified exact or adequately controlled
finite-precision representation. Its guarantee must hold for its
actual output, without an unverified random choice.

The interesting ranges have the displayed target substantially larger
than $\sqrt M$; otherwise this does not break the usual coherence
barrier. An identity matrix with $M=N$ therefore does not answer the
question. Likewise, a construction good for one specified sparse
vector is insufficient: the same matrix must preserve all sparse
vectors of the required order. State explicitly which pairs $(M,N)$
the construction supports and any padding needed for other dimensions.
\subsection{Short English statement}
Construct explicit deterministic restricted-isometry matrices with sparsity of order M to the three-fifths power, up to logarithmic factors.

\section{Attempt and results}
The preserved definition requires $M<N$ and sparsity substantially above
$\sqrt M$, but does not fix a lower bound on $N/M-1$. This matters:
near-square matrices give a literal easy family. The following construction
is stated with its precise dimensions; it is not claimed as resolution of
the intended compressed-sensing problem at fixed aspect ratio.

\paragraph{An exact deleted-Fourier construction.}
Take $N=M+1$, $M\ge1$, and
$A_{rj}=M^{-1/2}\exp(2\pi i rj/N)$ for $1\le r\le M$ and $0\le j<N$.
The geometric-series identity gives
\[
A^*A=\frac NM I_N-\frac1M J_N.
\]
On a support $J$ of cardinality $t$, the Gram error is
$M^{-1}(I_t-J_t)$. For $t\ge2$ its eigenvalues are $(1-t)/M$ on the
constant vector and $1/M$ on its orthogonal complement; for $t=1$ it is zero.
Consequently $\delta_s(A)=(s-1)/M$ for every integer $1\le s\le N$.
In particular $s\le1+M/3$ gives the required $1/3$ distortion, including
$s$ of order $M^{3/5}$. Entries have an exact succinct algebraic description
using a root of unity and a square root, and the output has $MN$ entries.
This is the usual simplex Gram matrix, an elementary known construction.
There is no claim of a floating-point certificate without error control.

\paragraph{How much codimension this method permits.}
More generally delete $r=N-M$ rows from the unitary $N$-point Fourier matrix
and scale the remaining rows by $\sqrt{N/M}$. Each column has norm one.
For distinct columns the full Fourier inner product is zero, so the retained
inner product is minus the deleted sum, of absolute value at most $r/M$.
For any $t$-sparse $x$, therefore,
\[
|\|Ax\|^2-\|x\|^2|
\le\frac rM\sum_{i\ne j}|x_i||x_j|
\le\frac rM(t-1)\|x\|^2.
\]
The last step is Cauchy--Schwarz: $(\sum |x_i|)^2\le t\sum|x_i|^2$.
Thus $r\le M^{2/5}$ allows $s$ of order $M^{3/5}$ by this certificate,
but only with $N/M\longrightarrow1$.

\paragraph{Why coherence alone stops at fixed aspect ratio.}
Let $N$ unit columns lie in $\mathbb C^M$, and let $\mu$ be their maximum
off-diagonal inner-product magnitude. If $G=A^*A$ has nonzero eigenvalues
$\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_q$, $q\le M$, then
$\sum\lambda_i=N$ and Cauchy--Schwarz gives
$\operatorname{tr}(G^2)\ge N^2/M$.
On the other hand $\operatorname{tr}(G^2)\le N+N(N-1)\mu^2$.
Hence
\[
\mu^2\ge\frac{N-M}{M(N-1)}.
\]
If $N\ge(1+c)M$ for fixed $c>0$, this is at least
$c/((1+c)M)$. A proof relying only on $(s-1)\mu\le1/3$ can then certify
only $s\le1+\frac13\sqrt{(1+c)M/c}$. This limits this sufficient test;
it is not an upper bound on the actual RIP order of every matrix.
