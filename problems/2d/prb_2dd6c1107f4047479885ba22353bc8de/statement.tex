\subsection{Definitions and mathematical statement}
In the standard graph-theoretic usage, for an integer $m\geq3$ the Henson graph $H_m$ is the countably infinite homogeneous $K_m$-free graph whose finite induced subgraphs, up to isomorphism, are exactly all finite $K_m$-free graphs. Here $K_m$ is the complete graph on $m$ vertices, $K_m$-free means containing no such clique, and homogeneous means that every isomorphism between finite induced subgraphs extends to an automorphism. The source neither selects $m$ nor states that this standard family, rather than another Henson structure, is the intended one.

A precise finite-model-property question must specify a language $\mathcal L$, a theory $T$, a class of allowed sentences, a class of finite structures, and the convention for finite models. Two distinct possible conventions illustrate the omission. One is finite satisfiability of each sentence true in a fixed structure $H$:
\[
\forall\varphi\text{ an }\mathcal L\text{-sentence},\quad
H\models\varphi\ \Longrightarrow\ 
\exists M\text{ finite}:\ M\models\varphi.
\]
This is the usual pseudofiniteness formulation when $H$ is infinite. One could instead require finite $K_m$-free graph witnesses, which is an additional restriction on $M$. Another theory-relative convention is
\[
\forall\varphi\text{ an allowed }\mathcal L\text{-sentence},\quad
T\cup\{\varphi\}\text{ satisfiable}\ \Longrightarrow\ 
\exists M\text{ finite}:\ M\models T\cup\{\varphi\}.
\]
These formulations impose different requirements; in particular requiring a finite model of an entire theory containing infinitude axioms is different from requiring separate finite models of individual sentences of that theory. A fragment of logic or a different associated theory could yield still another question.
