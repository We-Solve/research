\section{Literature and context check}

I read Nadler's GNMR talk on pages 1383--1384 of the primary report and the
Zilber--Nadler ICML paper's official abstract. The report explicitly distinguishes
quadratic local convergence from the condition-number dependence in recovery
conditions. The abstract concerns inductive completion and does not by itself
supply a condition-number-free theorem for ordinary completion. The following
fully observed diagonal calculation is a controlled auxiliary case; it isolates
one source of conditioning but does not replace random entry sampling.

\section{Research attempt: Balanced-factor conditioning and a relative Newton calculation}

\paragraph{Where a factor gradient iteration retains the condition number.}
Let $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r>0$, $r\geq1$, and consider
\[
 F(u,v)=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^r(u_iv_i-\lambda_i)^2.
\]
This is the diagonal fully observed factorization problem.
Gradient descent preserves the subspace $u=v=a$ and on it has iteration
\[
 a_i^+=a_i-\eta(a_i^2-\lambda_i)a_i.
\]
At the positive solution $a_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}$, the derivative of this update
is $1-2\eta\lambda_i$. Write $L=\max\lambda_i$, $\mu=\min\lambda_i$,
and $\kappa=L/\mu$. Any fixed step size has worst linearized absolute factor
at least
\[
 \max\{|1-2\eta\mu|,|1-2\eta L|\}
 \geq \frac{L-\mu}{L+\mu}.
\]
For proof, if both absolute values were smaller than a number $\rho$, then
$\eta>(1-\rho)/(2\mu)$ and $\eta<(1+\rho)/(2L)$.
These inequalities require $\rho>(L-\mu)/(L+\mu)$.
Equality is attained at $\eta=1/(L+\mu)$, and all intermediate eigenvalues
have absolute factor at most the endpoint maximum because an affine function
on an interval has its maximal absolute value at an endpoint.
Thus for large $\kappa$ even the best fixed scalar step has local factor
$(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$, approaching one. This statement concerns the
linearization of this specified gradient method, not all factorization methods.

\paragraph{A normalization that removes that factor in this toy model.}
For positive $a_i$ instead use the scalar Newton step
\[
 a_i^+=\frac12\left(a_i+\frac{\lambda_i}{a_i}\right).
\]
Let $r_i=(a_i-\sqrt{\lambda_i})/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$. Exact algebra gives
\[
 r_i^+=\frac{r_i^2}{2(1+r_i)}.
\]
If $|r_i|\leq1/2$, then $0\leq r_i^+\leq r_i^2\leq1/4$; by induction the
relative errors converge quadratically, with a constant independent of
$\kappa$. This route succeeds only in a relative neighborhood: the initial
absolute error allowed in the smallest component is proportional to
$\sqrt\mu$. The update also divides by $a_i$, so it is undefined at zero.
For a perturbed datum $\lambda_i+e_i$, the limiting positive root, when it
exists, obeys
\[
 \frac{\sqrt{\lambda_i+e_i}-\sqrt{\lambda_i}}{\sqrt{\lambda_i}}
 =\frac{e_i/\lambda_i}{\sqrt{1+e_i/\lambda_i}+1}.
\]
Thus absolute data noise has relative scale $e_i/\lambda_i$, and the
condition-number issue has not vanished from initialization or stability.

\paragraph{The sampling obstruction cannot be removed by rescaling factors.}
Suppose only the two diagonal entries of
\[
 X_t=\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\t^{-1}&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad t>0,
\]
are observed. Every $X_t$ has rank one: its second row equals $t^{-1}$ times
its first. Its condition number restricted to nonzero singular values is
therefore one. Yet all these different matrices produce the same observations.
No algorithm can recover all of them from those observations, regardless of
factor normalization or local convergence rate. A full recovery theorem must
control sampling identifiability, for example through a suitable injectivity
condition on the relevant matrix differences; the relative Newton calculation
does not establish that condition.

\section{Verification and adversarial audit}

The gradient-rate statement is explicitly a local derivative bound on the
balanced diagonal invariant subspace. At $\kappa=1$ its optimal factor is zero,
as the formula predicts; it is not asserted as a nonzero lower bound then.
Newton's relative identity is exact and includes its positive-denominator
hypothesis. The indistinguishable sampled matrices have rank exactly one for
every $t>0$, so their nonzero-singular-value condition number really is one.

\paragraph{Audit corrections.} Separated convergence rate from the relative size of its basin and from
statistical identifiability. The failure example uses the condition number over
nonzero singular values, avoiding an artificial infinite condition number for a
rank-one matrix.
