\subsection*{Definitions and mathematical statement}
A discounted perfect-information stochastic game has a nonempty finite state set $S$, a controlling player $c(s)\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$ at each state, nonempty finite actions $A(s)$ for its controller, rational payoffs $r_i(s,a)$ and rational transition probabilities $P(s'\mid s,a)\geq0$ with $\sum_{s'\in S}P(s'\mid s,a)=1$, and rational $\gamma\in(0,1)$. The current state and all past states and chosen actions are observed by every player. All data are explicitly listed in binary. A stationary profile is $x(\cdot\mid s)\in\Delta(A(s))$. Its normalized discounted payoff from $s$ is
\[
 V_i^x(s)=(1-\gamma)\mathbb E_{s,x}\sum_{t\geq0}\gamma^t r_i(s_t,a_t).
\]
The exact search relation asks for $x$ that is a Nash equilibrium from every initial state against arbitrary behavioral unilateral deviations. Equivalently, with
$r_i^x(s)=\sum_a x(a\mid s)r_i(s,a)$ and
$P^x(s'\mid s)=\sum_a x(a\mid s)P(s'\mid s,a)$, it asks for real $x$ satisfying the simplex constraints for which there exists $V\in\R^{n\times S}$ satisfying, for every $i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$ and $s\in S$, the first equation below, and for every $s\in S$ and $a\in A(s)$, the second inequality:
\begin{align*}
 V_i(s)&=(1-\gamma)r_i^x(s)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P^x(s'\mid s)V_i(s'),\\
 (1-\gamma)r_{c(s)}(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)V_{c(s)}(s')&\leq V_{c(s)}(s)
 \quad(s\in S,\ a\in A(s)).
\end{align*}
Classify this exact search problem for $n\geq3$, with fixed-$n$ subcases distinguished; in particular, determine whether the source's FIXP upper bound can be matched by FIXP-hardness under the corresponding exact fixed-point reductions.
\subsection*{Short English statement}
What is the exact complexity of stationary equilibrium computation once the discounted game has at least three players?
\subsection*{Variants and scope boundaries}
Exact solutions can be irrational, so an ordinary finite rational-output requirement is not adequate. Here FIXP uses exact fixed points of continuous self-maps of nonempty compact rational polytopes, represented by algebraic circuits over $\{+,-,\times,\div,\max,\min\}$ with rational constants, with every gate defined throughout the domain. The exact search and hardness conventions are those of [S2]: reductions transform instances in polynomial time and map every solution back by a separable affine map, each output coordinate having the form $\alpha_j y_{k(j)}+\beta_j$, with rational coefficients and coordinate indices computable from the original instance in polynomial time. This is a real-valued search relation, without an additional requirement to output a finite symbolic encoding of each real coordinate. The requested solution is $x$; $V$ is an auxiliary witness. Bit approximation and approximate equilibrium are different search relations. Every-state optimality is part of the source convention. The number of players, discount encoding, and action counts cannot be hidden when stating a reduction.
\subsection*{Source relationship and status}
[S1] already proves PPAD-completeness for the two-player case (Theorem 1). It leaves the precise complexity for three or more players open, with irrational examples and a four-player SqrtSum-hardness result (Theorem 3). Section 6 identifies possible FIXP-hardness as a remaining question. The original unrestricted ``complexity unresolved'' wording is therefore corrected.

\subsection*{Common conventions: Source mathematical conventions}

\textbf{Finite games.} For a finite set $A$, $\Delta(A)$ is its probability
simplex. Players choose independent mixed strategies in Nash equilibrium;
correlation is allowed only when explicitly part of the solution concept.
In a game with payoff functions $u_i$ and strategy profile $x$, an additive
$\varepsilon$ Nash equilibrium satisfies
\[
 u_i(x)\geq u_i(x_i',x_{-i})-\varepsilon
 \quad\text{for every player $i$ and every admissible deviation $x_i'$}.
\]
For numerical approximation, payoffs are normalized as locally specified.
An $\varepsilon$ payoff residual is distinct from distance at most
$\varepsilon$ to the set of exact equilibria. Point convergence, convergence
of empirical play, and convergence in distance to an equilibrium set are
also distinct.

\textbf{Computational representations.} Unless stated otherwise, a complexity
question takes rational data with binary encoding; polynomial time is measured
in total bit length. A fixed-accuracy polynomial algorithm is not necessarily
polynomial in $1/\varepsilon$, and neither is necessarily polynomial in
$\log(1/\varepsilon)$. Succinct games and value, demand, or sampling oracles
must declare their interfaces. Exact real solutions require an explicit
representation; an unspecified list of arbitrary real numbers is not a
finite computational output. A claimed algorithmic barrier is meaningful
only for its specified model and reductions.

\textbf{Dynamic games.} Histories, information, observation, and allowable
randomization are part of the model. A stationary strategy depends only on
the current state; a positional strategy in a deterministic graph game
chooses one action at each controlled vertex. Nash equilibrium at an initial
state and equilibrium after every history are different requirements.
An expectation of a pathwise $\liminf$ payoff, a $\liminf$ of expected averages,
a limiting expected average, and a uniform finite-horizon equilibrium are
different criteria. None is silently substituted for another.

\textbf{Allocation and mechanisms.} A complete allocation assigns every item
exactly once unless otherwise stated. Zero-valued goods, negative values,
capacity constraints, feasibility, lotteries, and copies of goods affect
fairness definitions and are specified locally. Dominant-strategy incentive
compatibility, Bayesian incentive compatibility, universal truthfulness,
and truthfulness in expectation impose different inequalities. Whenever
an objective ratio has zero denominator, its convention is stated or those
instances are excluded.

\textbf{Infinite-dimensional models.} Expectations, integrals, stochastic
equations, and optimization objectives require their local regularity,
integrability, filtrations, and admissible domains. A backward--forward
mean-field system is a boundary-value problem; it is not an initial-value
semiflow without an additional construction. Long-time, large-population,
and vanishing-noise limits require an order of limits and a convergence
topology. If a minimum need not be attained, the objective is its infimum
or supremum and the approximation requirement is stated separately.
